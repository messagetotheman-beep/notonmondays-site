What clients need before they contact a law firm

Most legal websites explain services. Few help people feel ready to take the first step. This concept explores what a calmer, clearer digital journey looks like in practice.

May 2026 · 5 min read · Service Design · Legal Sector

On this page

  • The concept
  • Selected moments
  • What changed
  • See it in practice

People do not arrive at a legal website ready to act. They arrive anxious, uncertain, and often unsure whether they even have the right kind of problem for the right kind of firm.

The barrier is rarely cost. It is usually clarity.

Most legal websites are organised around the firm's internal structure, not around how a client thinks about their situation. We explored what the alternative looks like in practice.

The Latchmere concept

We created a fictional family law firm to explore what a calmer, clearer digital journey could look like for someone approaching legal services during a difficult period. Every design decision is grounded in real behaviour and operational thinking. Each section of the demonstration carries an annotation explaining why the decision was made, not just what the decision was.

The firm does not exist. The problems it addresses do.

On the word "fictional" +

Latchmere is a design exploration, not a mockup of a client. It was built to give the thinking somewhere to live. The decisions it makes are the same decisions any legal firm faces when considering how to improve first-contact experience. The firm name and branding are fictional. The problems it addresses are not.

Latchmere concept homepage: situation-led navigation and reassurance-first hero

The Latchmere homepage. The hero addresses the user's emotional state, not the firm's service list. Navigation is built around situations, not legal categories.

Selected moments from the concept

Three design decisions run through the whole demonstration. Each one addresses a specific point where conventional legal websites make it harder for people to take the next step.

Service finder: situation-led cards including "divorce and separation", "buying or selling a home", and "not sure where to start"

The service finder. Categories are named for the user's situation, not the firm's legal specialisms.

Most users do not arrive knowing which legal service they need.

The service finder uses situation language throughout. Cards are named for what the user is experiencing: "my marriage is ending," "I need help with a home purchase." A prominent "not sure where to start" card removes the anxiety of having to name the problem correctly before finding the right place.

The operational reasoning +

Conventional navigation reflects the firm's internal structure: Family Law, Conveyancing, Employment, Wills. This is logical for people who already understand how legal services work. For people mid-situation, it adds cognitive load at exactly the wrong moment. Service-first navigation requires users to self-diagnose using legal terminology before they can find help. Situation-first navigation removes that requirement entirely.

Divorce and separation service page: reassurance-first headline and two-route CTA

Divorce and separation. The headline names the feeling, not the firm's expertise.

Before someone contacts a firm, they are asking whether it is safe to ask.

The service page opens with: "You do not have to work this out on your own." This answers the question most visitors are silently carrying before they read a word about the firm. Two CTAs offer different routes: one for people ready to act, one for people who need more information first.

Credibility versus reassurance +

Credentials build credibility. Reassurance builds permission. These are different things. A firm's accreditations and years of experience tell a potential client the firm is qualified. They do not tell the client whether it is safe to call without knowing the right legal terminology. Most firms lead with credibility. This is necessary but not sufficient. Reassurance copy directly addresses the specific anxiety that keeps people from taking the first step.

Contact page: reassurance copy and fee transparency placed before the form fields

The contact journey. Fee information appears before the form. The copy says: you do not need the right legal words.

Fee anxiety is present before the form appears. Addressing it early removes an unspoken reason to leave.

The contact page opens with: "Tell us what is happening. You do not need the right legal words." Fee transparency comes before the form fields. The experience treats the user as still deciding, not already committed.

Sequencing and emotional friction +

Most contact pages assume the decision to make contact has already been made. The form appears, the description field waits. For someone contacting about a divorce or redundancy, a blank "brief description" field asks them to articulate something they may not yet have words for, before they feel safe doing so. Showing fee information early, providing language scaffolding, and sequencing reassurance before data collection all address the same thing: an unspoken anxiety that causes people to leave rather than submit.

On the evidence

What shapes the moment of first contact

Visual impression

50ms

Users form a first visual impression of a website in under 50 milliseconds.

Lindgaard, G. et al., Behaviour and Information Technology, 2006

Design speaks before content does

The initial assessment of a professional services website happens before any copy is read. Visual clarity creates the conditions for trust.

Unmet legal need

~1 in 3

People with justiciable legal problems in England and Wales take no action to resolve them.

Legal Services Board, Legal Problem and Resolution Survey (consistent finding across multiple surveys)

The barrier is usually clarity

Sector research consistently shows that process uncertainty prevents people from seeking legal advice, often more than affordability does.

Design and credibility

46%

Of participants cited visual design as the primary basis for assessing a website's credibility.

Fogg, B.J. et al., Stanford Web Credibility Project, 2003

Presentation shapes perceived expertise

Users cannot directly evaluate a firm's legal expertise online. They evaluate its website. Design becomes a proxy for professional quality.

What changed, and why it matters

The differences are not primarily visual. What changes is the order, the language, and the assumption about who the user is when they arrive.

Conventional

Service-first navigation

Navigation reflects the firm's internal structure. Users must already know the legal category for their problem. Those who do not know are left to guess.

This concept

Situation-first navigation

Navigation reflects the user's experience. Users do not need to know the legal name for their situation. A prominent "not sure where to start" option removes the anxiety of getting it wrong.

Conventional

Expertise-led copy

Hero sections lead with the firm's credentials. This builds credibility but does not address the anxiety present at the moment of first contact.

This concept

Reassurance-led copy

"You do not have to work this out on your own." The reassurance acknowledges that this is hard, and that a clear next step is available without pressure.

Conventional

Form-first contact

Contact pages present a form with minimal guidance. Fee information is absent or buried. The page assumes the user is already ready to engage.

This concept

Guided contact journey

Reassurance and fee transparency come before the form. The copy says the user does not need the right legal words. The experience meets the user where they are.

The operational point

Clearer digital journeys produce more confident clients, better quality first enquiries, and a stronger relationship before the first conversation takes place. These are not aesthetic improvements. They address a commercial and operational problem that sits upstream of the legal service itself.
