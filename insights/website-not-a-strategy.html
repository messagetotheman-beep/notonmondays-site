A website is not a strategy

When a website is not working, the problem is rarely on the website.

May 2026 · 7 min read · Digital Experience · Operations
Share · X · LinkedIn ·

On this page

  • What the website is showing you
  • Navigation built from the inside out
  • Optimising for publishing, not understanding
  • What changes when organisations get clearer

TL;DR

  • Most website problems are organisational problems made visible online.
  • Navigation that reflects departments rather than user needs is a symptom, not a root cause.
  • Organisations that optimise for publishing content rarely understand what users actually need.
  • A clearer operating model tends to produce a clearer digital experience.
  • Fixing the website without fixing what sits behind it rarely holds.

When a website is not working, the first instinct is usually to redesign it.

That instinct is often wrong.

A website is one of the clearest windows into how an organisation actually operates. If it feels confused or hard to navigate, that is usually because something behind it is also confused. Not always on the surface. Not always obviously. But the patterns are there if you know what to look for.

The friction that shows up in a digital experience rarely starts there. It starts in how ownership is divided, how content is managed, how enquiries are handled, and how clearly the organisation understands the people it is trying to serve.

What the website is showing you

Navigation that groups services by department rather than by user problem. Content that covers every eventuality but helps nobody move forward. A contact page buried three levels deep because nobody agreed on how to handle inbound enquiries.

None of that is a design decision. It is an operational one.

Website problems tend to cluster around the same internal fractures: unclear ownership, unclear purpose, unclear users. When those things are settled, the experience tends to get better. When they are not, they show up on the page.

Who owns what

Fragmented digital experiences almost always have fragmented ownership behind them. Multiple teams publishing content independently, with no shared understanding of what the site is there to do. A CMS that has grown organically for years without anyone checking whether the pages still serve a purpose.

The result is a site that reflects the organisational chart rather than the user's question.

The accumulation problem

Most organisations are much better at adding content than removing it. Publishing feels like progress. Removing feels like risk. So pages accumulate, navigation grows, and the user journey gets longer without getting clearer.

At some point, a redesign gets commissioned. New structure, new design, same underlying decisions. The same teams still own the same sections. The same content still gets added. The same clarity is still missing.

Worth knowing

A redesign can solve a symptoms problem. It can make the experience feel better for a period. But if the organisational questions that created the confusion are not addressed, the friction tends to return. Different page, same problem.

Visibility rewards clarity

Search surfaces content that is clear, consistent, and answers genuine questions. Organisational complexity works against all three.

Navigation built from the inside out

Most navigation structures make sense to the people who built them.

There is a services section organised by business unit. A news section that is mostly internal announcements. A resources tab containing everything that did not fit elsewhere.

Users arrive with a question. They are looking for help with a specific thing. They are not arriving to understand the company's structure or vocabulary.

When navigation mirrors internal departments, it forces users to translate. They have to map their problem onto the organisation's language before they can find anything. Most do not bother. They leave, search again, or try a competitor.

The language gap

Copy on most corporate websites is written for the organisation. It describes what the company does in the company's terms. Internal product names, internal process language, internal category structures.

Users search in plain language. They describe their problem, not the solution they expect to receive. When the gap between those two vocabularies is too wide, nothing connects. The user does not find the page, or finds it and does not recognise themselves in it.

Whose job is it to close that gap

Closing it is not primarily a copywriting task. It requires genuine understanding of who the user is and what they need at each stage of the journey. That understanding is usually held in fragments across the organisation, if it is held at all.

Teams responsible for the website rarely have access to the conversations that would tell them how users actually talk about their problems. Sales teams do. Support teams do. Often that knowledge does not reach the people writing the content.

The most revealing question to ask about a website is not what it contains. It is who it is actually written for.

Optimising for publishing, not understanding

Content teams are usually measured on output. Articles published, pages updated, campaigns sent. The question of whether any of it reaches the right person at the right moment is rarely asked systematically.

This is how organisations end up with hundreds of pages and no clear signal about whether any of them are doing a job.

Good content is not about volume. It is about whether something answers the question a user actually has, at the moment they have it. A single clear page that helps someone move forward is worth more than twenty that do not.

Search and the clarity reward

Search engines have become considerably better at understanding intent. They surface content that answers genuine questions clearly, and they have become less forgiving of content that is technically optimised but practically useless.

Organisations that understand their users well tend to write more clearly. That clarity is what makes content findable. Good search visibility starts before SEO and before any campaign. It starts with knowing what you are trying to say and who needs to hear it.

The journey after contact

A website is not where the user experience ends. It is usually where it begins.

What happens after someone makes contact is often where the real friction lives. Enquiries routed to the wrong team. Information the user already provided asked for again. Response times that are inconsistent because nobody owns the intake process end to end.

These are not website problems. They are systems problems. And they tend to surface long before anyone starts talking about the systems themselves. The website exposes the gap; it does not create it.

What changes when organisations get clearer

The digital experience tends to improve when the operating model improves. Not as a byproduct of a website project. As a consequence of getting clearer on the fundamentals.

Who the users are. What they actually need at each stage. Who is responsible for each part of the journey. What good looks like when someone gets in touch and what happens next.

Organisations that do this work tend to find the website becomes easier to fix as a result. Not because the design changes, but because there is a shared understanding of what the site is supposed to do. That understanding makes decisions easier, content clearer, and ownership less contested.

The connection between internal clarity and external experience

A website that is clear about what it offers, who it serves, and what to do next tends to reflect an organisation that is equally clear about those things internally.

That clarity does not come from the website. It comes from the operating layer that sits underneath it. The decisions, processes, and ownership that determine how work actually moves. When that layer is healthy, the surface tends to follow.

Where to start

The most useful question to ask when a website is not working is not "what should we change on the site?"

It is "what is this showing us about how we operate?"

That question usually leads somewhere more useful than another redesign brief.

Common signals that the problem is not the website +
  • Navigation sections that map directly to internal teams or job titles
  • Multiple teams publishing to the same areas of the site with no clear ownership
  • Contact forms that ask for information the team then asks for again
  • Pages that exist because someone wanted them, not because users need them
  • No consistent view of which content is performing and which is not
  • Enquiries that arrive but do not convert, with no systematic understanding of why
  • A redesign that went live but did not change the underlying metrics
