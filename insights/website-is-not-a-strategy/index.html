A website is not a strategy

Most organisations treat a poor website as the problem. It is usually where deeper issues become visible.

June 2026 · 6 min read · Digital · Operations
Share · X · LinkedIn ·

On this page

  • The brief that reveals the problem
  • What the website actually reflects
  • What organisations that perform well have in common
  • Where to start

TL;DR

  • A poor website is rarely the root cause. It usually reflects unclear ownership, fragmented content, or disconnected systems.
  • A new website may improve appearances. It rarely fixes the underlying causes.
  • Organisations with strong digital performance tend to have clarity in operations, content, and data. Not just a better build.
  • The question worth asking first is not "do we need a new website?" It is "why is this one not working?"

At some point, most leadership teams have a version of the same conversation.

The website is not good enough. It does not reflect the organisation. Enquiries are low. Clients cannot find what they need. Something needs to change.

The conclusion is often the same: commission a new website.

Sometimes that is the right call. Often, it is not. Because the website is not the problem. It is where the problem has become visible.

The brief that reveals the problem

When organisations sit down to brief a website project, a pattern tends to emerge.

The brief describes symptoms: outdated design, poor mobile experience, confusing navigation, not enough enquiries. But when you ask what should replace it, clarity drops quickly.

Who owns the content? Which services should be featured and how? What should a user do when they arrive? What happens after they make an enquiry? How is the site maintained once it is live?

These questions are harder than they appear. Not because the organisation lacks intelligence, but because the answers require decisions that no website project can make for them.

Worth knowing

The Government Digital Service identified unclear content ownership as one of the most common reasons government websites degrade after launch. The same pattern is consistent across professional services organisations of all sizes.

When a brief cannot answer these questions, the new website usually inherits the same problems as the old one. Better design. Same confusion underneath.

What the website actually reflects

A website does not exist in isolation. It reflects the organisation that built and maintains it.

In professional services, the issues that show up on the surface most often trace back to one or more of the following.

Unclear ownership

Content is owned collectively, which in practice means no one owns it. Pages go stale. Services are described differently in different places. The person who knows what should be there is not the person responsible for the site.

Fragmented services

Organisations grow and their offer becomes more complex. The website tries to describe everything but ends up explaining nothing well. Users cannot tell what the organisation actually does, or whether it does the specific thing they need.

Disconnected systems

The enquiry form submits to an inbox. The inbox is shared. No one is quite sure what happens next. Client data lives in spreadsheets, a CRM, and several email threads simultaneously. The website cannot be useful if the systems behind it are not connected.

This is something search visibility research consistently supports: organisations invest in driving traffic to experiences that cannot convert it, because the operational layer behind the website has not been thought through.

Inconsistent processes

What the website promises and what the organisation actually delivers diverge in ways that are hard to manage from the outside. Response times vary. The experience of making contact depends on who picks up. The website says one thing; the reality is different.

Weak content governance

No one has agreed what the website is for, who should write for it, how often it is reviewed, or what getting better would look like. This is not a technology problem. It is an operating problem.

Nielsen Norman Group research consistently shows that users abandon websites not because of design, but because they cannot find answers to specific questions quickly. In most cases, the content to answer those questions exists somewhere in the organisation. It has simply never been organised for the people who need it.

What organisations that perform well have in common

The organisations with strong, consistent digital performance are not necessarily the ones who spent the most on their websites. They are the ones who sorted out the things the website depends on.

Clear ownership

Someone is responsible for the digital experience. Not a committee. Not "marketing and IT." A named person with the authority to make decisions about content, structure, and priorities.

Useful content

The content answers the questions users actually have, not the questions the organisation wishes they had. It is specific, current, and reviewed regularly. It is not written to impress; it is written to be useful.

Trusted data

When a user makes an enquiry, that information goes somewhere reliable. It is logged, owned, and acted on consistently. The people responsible for responding have what they need to do so. Progress is visible.

The Salesforce State of the Connected Customer report has noted repeatedly that the majority of customers expect organisations to present as one connected entity, not a collection of siloed departments. When data is fragmented across systems, that experience becomes difficult to deliver regardless of how good the website looks.

Joined-up systems

The website is connected to the operational systems behind it. Enquiries flow into the right place. Client information is held in one place, not several. The people who manage relationships have visibility of what has happened.

This is not about having complex technology. It is about having a clear operating layer that connects the digital front of the organisation to the people and processes behind it.

Clear next steps for users

Every page has a purpose. Users know what to do and how to do it. The route from finding the organisation to making contact is short, obvious, and works.

Regular improvement

The website is not treated as a project with an end date. It is treated as a service that requires ongoing attention. Someone reviews it. Someone acts on what the data shows. Something changes as a result.

Where to start

If a leadership team is considering a new website, the most useful thing to do first is not to brief an agency. It is to understand why the current one is not working.

That conversation usually surfaces the real issues quickly.

  • Who currently owns the website and what does their ownership include?
  • What happens when a user makes an enquiry today?
  • Which parts of the site reflect the organisation accurately and which do not?
  • What systems are connected to the site and which should be?
  • What would need to be true for the site to work well in twelve months' time?

The answers to these questions tend to reveal whether the problem is the website or the organisation behind it.

Often, the honest answer is both. The site needs improvement and so do some of the things it depends on. Knowing which is which changes what you should do first.

A new website built on the same unclear foundations will, within eighteen months, look much like the one it replaced.

The organisations that get lasting value from digital investment are the ones that treat it as an operational question, not a design one. They ask what needs to be true inside the organisation for the website to do its job. Then they fix that, and build around it.

The website is often the most visible part of a professional services organisation. It is worth making good. But making it good requires being honest about what is underneath it.

It may not be a website problem at all. It may be a clarity problem.
